What can the chain of Oral Torah tell us?

Avot, Rambam’s forty transmission positions, and evidence for inherited law.

Explore this study in the atlas

Two ways of telling a transmission history

Avot opens with Moshe, Yehoshua, elders, prophets, and the Men of the Great Assembly. Rambam’s introduction to the Mishneh Torah traces forty transmission positions from Moshe to Rav Ashi, with some positions occupied by more than one named person. These accounts tell us how rabbinic tradition understands continuity. Each pair of transmitters raises its own historical question, including how to understand Rambam’s link from Rava to Rav Ashi. Avot 1:1 · Rambam, Transmission of the Oral Law

Follow a practice as well as a name

Josephus reports that the Pharisees passed on inherited regulations outside the written laws of Moshe and that the Sadducees disputed their validity. This attests to an ancient dispute over inherited law. We can test the details by following a practice. Vayikra commands self-denial on Yom Kippur. Mishnah Yoma identifies eating, drinking, bathing and other activities as prohibited. Those sources let us ask how the practice became specified and what earlier witnesses can tell us about it. Josephus, Antiquities 13.297 · Vayikra 16:29 · Mishnah Yoma 8:1

The forty positions also invite a Kabbalistic question about four worlds and ten sefirot. That is a structural reading of the number. Any gematria of the chain needs an explicit list, stable spellings, and rules for titles and paired names before its result can be evaluated.
