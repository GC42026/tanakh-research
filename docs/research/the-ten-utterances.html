Why call them the Ten Utterances?

The Torah counts ten utterances. What changes when we count commands?

Explore this study in the atlas

What the Hebrew calls them

Shemot 34:28 and Devarim 4:13 call them עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים, the ten words, statements, or utterances. Devarim calls them the covenant and says YHVH commanded Israel to perform it. “Ten Commandments” brings out the binding force of the passage. “Ten Utterances” preserves the Torah’s way of naming and counting divine speech. Shemot 34:28 · Devarim 4:13

The first-person declaration “I am YHVH your Elohim” introduces the speaker through the Exodus. Later utterances contain several distinct imperatives. Even when a tradition identifies a mitzvah in the opening declaration, counting the speech units and enumerating legal duties are different operations. The versions in Shemot and Devarim also give different reasons for Shabbat: Creation in the first, slavery and redemption in the second. Shemot 20:1–14 · Devarim 5:6–18

What the title leaves open

Berakhot describes the Ten Utterances being recited in the Temple and the abandonment of a proposed daily recitation elsewhere because of the claims of sectarians. The report shows that the liturgical place of the ten mattered. The genealogy of the English title is a separate historical question, best traced through particular translations and editions before assigning motives. Berakhot 12a
