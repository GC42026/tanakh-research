What does a date in Jewish history measure?

Calendars, remembered events, and the Persian-period discrepancy.

Where the timelines diverge

Avodah Zarah assigns 34 years of Persian rule during the Second Temple’s existence. Historians reconstruct a much longer Persian period in the region. Cyrus’s capture of Babylon in 539 BCE is one anchor for the historical chronology. The two figures require matching start and end points: the years of Persian rule over a standing Temple are a different measure from the full span of an empire. Even with those boundaries made explicit, a substantial discrepancy remains. Avodah Zarah 9a · Encyclopaedia Iranica, The Achaemenid Period · Torat Har Etzion, The Kings of Persia and the Missing Years

A day can mark more than a date

Melakhim dates Nevuzaradan’s arrival to the seventh day of the fifth month. Yirmeyahu gives the tenth. Ta’anit describes a sequence that takes in the seventh, ninth, and tenth, and explains the fast on the ninth through the onset of destruction. Zechariah already speaks of fasting in the fifth month and asks what the fast is for. The sequence, the date observed, and the meaning of observing it are three questions that can be investigated together. II Melakhim 25:8–9 · Yirmeyahu 52:12–13 · Ta’anit 29a · Zechariah 7:5–10
