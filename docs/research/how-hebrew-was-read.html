How did readers know which vowels to use?

Masoretic pointing, oral reading, and what a single word can show.

The writing and the reading

Readers had oral traditions, context, grammar, and consonantal letters that sometimes represented vowels. The medieval Tiberian signs record a reading tradition in detail. Other systems, including Babylonian and Palestinian pointing, are witnesses to the variety of ways biblical Hebrew was transmitted. A date for the notation and a date for a particular pronunciation are different historical claims. Geoffrey Khan, The Tiberian Pronunciation Tradition of Biblical Hebrew

At the end of Bereishit 47, the consonants מטה can be read as mittah, “bed,” or matteh, “staff.” The Masoretic reading gives Yaakov a bed in this verse. The ancient Greek translation gives him a staff. One set of consonants can thus carry two sharply different scenes. The Greek rendering is evidence for how the translator understood the passage. The question of how that understanding arose remains open. Bereishit 47:31 · Raymond de Hoop, “Then Israel Bowed Himself…”

What to compare next

Record each consonantal form, its transmitted reading, and the rendering of any ancient witness that actually preserves the passage. Compare them before making a claim about which interpretation is older. Where the consonants themselves differ, as in some witnesses to Jeremiah, record that as a different kind of evidence.
